IPO subscription-ratio framework · Nov 2026

Reading the IPO book

How a question about one SME IPO turned into a framework for reading who is in an IPO book, why I backtested it instead of trusting it, and the logic behind each step.

For research purposes only. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to apply for, buy or sell any security.

StartThe question

Would a ₹2.5 lakh ticket scare retail away?

Maxwell Engineering Solutions came to the NSE SME platform at ₹150 to ₹158 with a lot of 800 shares. Retail had to apply for 2 lots, so the minimum application was ₹2,52,800. My starting hypothesis was simple: a ticket that large should keep small investors out and leave the issue undersubscribed.

The goal was to use the subscription numbers by category (QIB, NII, retail, in "x" multiples) to predict how it would list on 6 November.

Turn 1Hypothesis breaks

Retail came anyway. The gap was somewhere else.

By day 3 retail was 219x subscribed, with more than 94,000 applications by day 2. The lot size did nothing to demand. In SME issues the "retail" bucket is effectively a mini-HNI bucket, and a lot of it is funded, so a ₹2.5 lakh minimum is not a barrier for the people who actually apply.

What stood out instead was the institutional side. QIB was at 0x through day 2. The book was big, but almost nobody who prices risk for a living was in it.

The question changed from "how big is the book?" to "who is in the book?"
LogicRatios

Turning "who is in the book" into numbers

Each category multiple says how hungry one type of investor is. Dividing one by another says whose money is driving the book. I used five ratios:

EvidenceComparables

What good books looked like

Mainboard. The recent big listings were all led by institutions. Jio Platforms (QIB 70x, RII/QIB 0.04x) listed +21%. Rentomojo (QIB 177x, RII/QIB 0.09x) listed +19%, after QIB jumped from 0.45x on day 2 to 177x on day 3. BLS Polymers (QIB 200x) listed +65% on 3 November. The pattern: retail small relative to QIB, listing around +20% or better, and the gain held.

SME. I pulled the best SME books of 2026 and a few flops. The winners (Gallard, Current Infraprojects, Exato, Shyam Dhani, Austere, BLT) all had QIB above 80x, RII/QIB between 1.5x and 8x and a total above 350x. The flops (Shellz, Core4, Wiscon) all had a total below 60x. Core4 also had QIB near zero with NII/RII above 4x, and listed at -20%.

Maxwell did not fit either group. Its RII/QIB was 62x, about eight times the most retail-heavy winner, and its total of 168x sat between the flops and the winners.

The call on Maxwell from this framework: a listing pop driven by GMP (₹70 to ₹80, 44% to 50%), but the highest fade risk of anything I had looked at.

Turn 2Why backtest

Ten examples are a story, not evidence

Three things made me distrust my own rules:

  • Selection bias. The SME winners came from a "highest subscription of 2026" list. Of course they had big books. I never saw the big books that flopped.
  • Conflicting outside evidence. SEBI found that subscription predicts listing gains on the mainboard but not on SME. Another analysis found 46% of 294 SME IPOs subscribed above 100x trade below issue.
  • The rules came from the same cases they explained. A rule only means something if it works on IPOs it was not built from.

So the job became: run every rule against every IPO I can get data for, keep SME and mainboard apart, and see which rules survive.

LogicBacktest design

How the test is built

Collect. SME 2017 to Jan 2024 and mainboard 2009 to 2023 from a public HuggingFace dataset (built from chittorgarh), all of 2026 from chittorgarh, and mainboard 2024 to 2025 from NSE data via Fidus. In total, 756 listed SME and 673 mainboard IPOs.
Use the numbers an investor sees before applying. Subscription is taken from live bids at the close of the issue, not the post-rejection allotment figures. For Core4 the two differ a lot: 5.06x QIB live against 2.71x after rejections.
Throw out numbers that are not final. The old mainboard data stores day-by-day snapshots that usually stop before the last day (Tata Technologies: 15x on day 2 against 69x final). Category ratios are kept only where they match the final total.
Keep SME and mainboard separate. Their investor bases, lot sizes and listing rules differ. NSE SME also caps the listing open at +90%.
Split in-sample from out-of-sample. 2026 is where the framework came from, so it is in sample. Everything before 2026 is the real test.
Score each rule on outcomes. Median listing open and close, the share that opened above issue, the share that opened +20% or more, and the share that closed below its own open (intraday fade).
Compare against simple baselines. Does a rule beat total subscription alone? Does anything beat GMP?

The biggest lesson: total subscription carries almost all the signal. A one-variable model on total subscription separates +20% listings from the rest with an AUC of 0.91 on 2026 data it never saw. Adding the ratios does not improve SME at all and improves mainboard only slightly (0.92 to 0.93). GMP, where available, already priced the book: listing opens landed on GMP on average.

The ratios do tell one real story. Their sign flips between segments. In SME, a heavier retail book goes with a bigger pop. On the mainboard, a QIB-led book does better. The framework had the mainboard right and the SME direction backwards.

Institutions came in on the last day, like Rentomojo. That moves Maxwell from the untested bucket into the strong one, which has listed at a median +90% with no losers in 14 cases. Its five closest historical books opened between +10% and +90%.

The lesson for the method: a book is only comparable to history once bidding has closed. QIB money arrives late, and day 2 ratios can be completely different from the final ones.

LogicThe scanner

Turning the backtest into a tool

The IPO Book Scanner takes any book, live or historical, and answers three questions:

  • Which framework bucket is it in, and how has that bucket actually listed? The same rules as the backtest, applied to the four multiples.
  • How did books of the same size list? Total-subscription bands (SME: under 10x, 10 to 60x, 60 to 100x, 100 to 350x, above 350x), since size turned out to be the strongest signal. It also gives the probability of a +20% open from a logistic model on log total subscription.
  • Which five past books looked most like it? Distance is measured on the logs of QIB, NII, retail and total, so 10x against 20x counts the same as 100x against 200x. The tool shows how those five listed.

A daily job at 18:00 IST, after bidding closes, re-scrapes the current year, pulls mainboard data from NSE, rebuilds the dataset, reruns the backtest and refreshes the scanner's data. Post-listing prices were dropped to keep the pipeline lean, so the tool covers listing day only.

OpenLimits

What this cannot tell me yet

  • SME 2024 and 2025 are missing. Past years sit behind a paywall and no free source has final SME category data for them.
  • Listing day only. Whether "fade" shows up over 30 days is untested.
  • Small buckets. The discount-risk and strong buckets have 5 and 14 cases.
  • Mainboard 2024 to 2025 uses NSE bids only, which run lower than combined NSE and BSE figures.
  • Next checks: record Carver Roboweld (4 Nov) and Maxwell (6 Nov) against the scanner's calls, and read Maxwell's RHP for other income, related-party sales and customer concentration before trusting the ~40% margins.
