Reading the IPO book

Not investment advice. Not SEBI registered. The author is not registered with SEBI as an investment adviser or research analyst. This is personal research for education only.

How one question about an SME IPO became a framework for reading who is in a book, why I backtested it instead of trusting it, and the logic behind each step.

01 The question

Would a ₹2.5 lakh ticket keep retail away?

Maxwell Engineering Solutions came to the NSE SME platform at ₹150 to ₹158 with a lot of 800 shares. Retail had to apply for 2 lots, so the minimum application was ₹2,52,800. The starting hypothesis: a ticket that large should keep small investors out and leave the issue undersubscribed.

The goal was to use subscription by category (QIB, NII, retail, in multiples) to predict how it would list on 6 November.

02 Hypothesis breaks

Retail came anyway. The gap was somewhere else.

By day 3 retail was 219x subscribed, with more than 94,000 applications by day 2. In SME issues the retail bucket behaves like a mini HNI bucket, much of it funded, so a ₹2.5 lakh minimum is no barrier for the people who actually apply.

What stood out was the institutional side. QIB sat at 0x through day 2. The book was big, but almost nobody who prices risk for a living was in it.

The question changed from "how big is the book?" to "who is in the book?"

04 Comparables

What good books looked like

Mainboard. Recent big listings were led by institutions. Jio Platforms (QIB 70x, RII/QIB 0.04x) listed +21%. Rentomojo (QIB 177x, RII/QIB 0.09x) listed +19% after QIB jumped from 0.45x on day 2 to 177x on day 3. BLS Polymers (QIB 200x) listed +65%. Retail small relative to QIB, listing near +20% or better, and the gain held.

SME. The best 2026 books (Gallard, Current Infraprojects, Exato, Shyam Dhani, Austere, BLT) all had QIB above 80x, RII/QIB between 1.5x and 8x and total above 350x. The flops (Shellz, Core4, Wiscon) all had total below 60x. Core4 also had QIB near zero with NII/RII above 4x and listed at -20%.

Maxwell fit neither group. Its RII/QIB was 62x, about eight times the most retail-heavy winner, and its total of 168x sat between flops and winners.

05 Framework

The working rules

The call on Maxwell from these rules: a GMP-driven pop (₹70 to ₹80, 44% to 50%) with the highest fade risk of anything looked at.

06 Why backtest

Ten examples are a story, not evidence

  • Selection bias. The SME winners came from a "highest subscription of 2026" list. The big books that flopped were never in view.
  • Conflicting evidence. SEBI found subscription predicts mainboard listing gains but not SME. Another study found 46% of 294 SME IPOs subscribed above 100x trade below issue.
  • Rules built from the cases they explain. A rule only means something if it works on IPOs it was not built from.

So: run every rule against every IPO with data, keep SME and mainboard apart, and see what survives.

07 Design

How the test is built

  1. Collect. SME 2017 to Jan 2024 and mainboard 2009 to 2023 from a public HuggingFace dataset, all of 2026 from chittorgarh, mainboard 2024 to 2025 from NSE via Fidus. 756 SME and 673 mainboard listings.
  2. Use what an investor sees before applying. Live bids at close, not post-rejection allotment figures. For Core4 these differ a lot: 5.06x QIB live against 2.71x after rejections.
  3. Drop numbers that are not final. Old mainboard data stores daily snapshots that often stop early (Tata Technologies: 15x on day 2 against 69x final). Ratios are kept only where they match the final total.
  4. Keep segments apart. Investor bases, lot sizes and listing rules differ. NSE SME also caps the listing open at +90%.
  5. Split in and out of sample. 2026 is where the rules came from. Everything earlier is the real test.
  6. Score on outcomes. Median open and close, share opening above issue, share opening +20% or more, share closing below its own open.
  7. Beat a baseline. Does a rule beat total subscription alone? Does anything beat GMP?

Total subscription carries almost all the signal. A one-variable model on total separates +20% listings from the rest with AUC 0.91 on 2026 data it never saw. Ratios add nothing on SME and little on mainboard (0.92 to 0.93). GMP, where available, already priced the book.

The ratios tell one real story: their sign flips. In SME a heavier retail book goes with a bigger pop. On mainboard a QIB-led book does better. The framework had mainboard right and the SME direction backwards.

Institutions came in on the last day, as with Rentomojo. That moves Maxwell into the strong bucket: median +90% with no losers in 14 cases. Its five closest books opened between +10% and +90%.

A book is only comparable to history once bidding has closed. QIB money arrives late, and day 2 ratios can look nothing like the final ones.

10 The scanner

From backtest to tool

  • Bucket. Which framework rule fires, and how that bucket has listed.
  • Size band. How books of similar total listed, plus the probability of a +20% open from a logistic model on log total.
  • Neighbours. The five past books closest on log QIB, NII, retail and total, so 10x against 20x counts the same as 100x against 200x.

A job at 18:00 IST on weekdays re-scrapes the current year, rebuilds the dataset, reruns the backtest and refreshes this site. It covers listing day only.

11 Limits

What this cannot tell yet

  • SME 2024 and 2025 are missing. No free source has final category data for them.
  • Listing day only. Fade over 30 days is untested.
  • Small buckets. 5 and 14 cases in the risk and strong buckets.
  • Mainboard 2024 to 2025 is NSE bids only, which run below combined NSE and BSE figures.
